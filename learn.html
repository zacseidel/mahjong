Learn American mah jongg

Everything you need to play your first hand here, in about ten minutes. Examples use this site’s own practice card; the game works with your own card too.

1. What American mah jongg is

Four players each try to be the first to collect 14 tiles that exactly match one of the hands printed on the card. In the American game, a new card of hands is published every year, and everyone at the table plays from the same card.

If you’ve seen other styles, the big differences are:

  • The card. You don’t build any hand you like; you build one of the hands on the card, exactly.
  • No runs are called. You can take someone’s discard only for a group of three or more identical tiles, or to win.
  • Jokers. Eight jokers can stand in for tiles in groups of three or more.
  • The Charleston. Before play starts, everyone passes tiles to each other to improve their hands.
  • Flowers are part of hands, not bonus tiles.

Winds (four of each)

3. Reading the card

Each line on the card is one hand. Here is one from the practice card:

Consecutive Run · 30 X · any 3 consecutive numbers
  • Groups are separated by spaces. 3333 is a kong (four of a kind), 222 a pung (three), 11 a pair.
  • Colors are suits, but not particular suits. Everything in the same color is in the same suit; different colors must be different suits. You choose which suits.
  • D is a dragon: the one paired with the suit of its color. Here the green groups are one suit, so the red D is the dragon of a different suit.
  • The note says which numbers may vary. “Any 3 consecutive” means 1-2-3, 2-3-4, and so on up to 7-8-9. Without a note, the numbers are exactly as printed.
  • X or C: X hands may be exposed (you may call discards to make groups). C hands must stay concealed.
  • The number is the hand’s value in points.

One way to make that hand, with Bams as the green suit and Craks for the red dragon:

A mixed group like is four single tiles: a 2, a Soap (for 0), another 2, and a 6, all 2s and 6s in one suit.

4. The Charleston

East starts with 14 tiles; everyone else has 13. Before play, everyone passes three tiles at a time, all at once. Jokers can never be passed.

  1. First Charleston (always): pass right, then across, then left.
  2. On that last pass (left), you may blind pass: pass along 1, 2, or 3 of the tiles coming to you without looking at them, and make up the rest from your hand. Useful when you don’t have three tiles you want to give up.
  3. Second Charleston (optional): anyone may stop it. If it happens: left, across, right. The last pass (right) may be blind.
  4. Courtesy pass: you and the player across from you each ask for 0 to 3 tiles, and trade the smaller number.

An easy way to remember: Right, Across, Left, then back the other way: Left, Across, Right.

Use the Charleston to choose which hands to aim for. Pass tiles that fit none of them.

5. A turn, and calling discards

East discards first. Then play moves to the right (East, South, West, North). On your turn you:

  1. Draw a tile from the wall.
  2. Optionally make joker exchanges (see Jokers).
  3. Declare Mah Jongg if you have it, or discard one tile face up and name it.

Calling

When someone discards, you may take (“call”) that tile:

  • to complete a group of three or more identical tiles, which you then place face up (“expose”), jokers allowed; or
  • to win. This is the only time you can call a tile for a pair or a single.
Holding 7 Bam, 7 Bam, and a joker, you can call a discarded 7 Bam and expose a kong.

After calling for an exposure, you discard, and play continues to your right; anyone in between is skipped. A discarded joker can’t be called. If more than one person wants a tile, a call to win beats a call to expose; otherwise the player next in turn gets it.

Exposing tells everyone what you’re building and rules out every concealed (C) hand. This site only offers you calls that still fit a hand on the card.

6. Jokers

  • A joker can stand in for any tile in a group of three or more: pung, kong, quint.
  • A joker can never be part of a pair or a single tile, including the single tiles in year hands and singles-and-pairs hands.
  • A quint (five of a kind) always needs a joker, since there are only four of each tile.

Joker exchange

On your turn, if any exposure on the table has a joker standing in for a tile you hold, you may swap your real tile for that joker.

Someone exposed a pung of North with a joker. If you hold a North, you can swap it for their joker.

A joker is usually worth more than the tile you give up, since it fits any group.

7. Winning, dead hands, and wall games

Mah Jongg: your 14 tiles (exposed plus concealed) exactly match one hand on the card, with jokers only in groups. You can win on a tile you draw (“self-drawn”) or on someone’s discard.

With training wheels on (the default), the Mah Jongg button lights up only when your hand really is Mah Jongg. With them off, declaring by mistake makes your hand dead.

Dead hand: a player whose exposures don’t fit any hand on the card, or who has the wrong number of tiles, can’t win. Their hand is “dead”: they stop playing, and still pay the winner.

Wall game: if the wall runs out before anyone wins, nobody pays. The deal passes to the next player either way.

8. Scoring

The winner collects from everyone else, based on the hand’s value on the card.

How you winWho pays
On a discardThe discarder pays double; the other two pay the value.
Self-drawnAll three pay double.
JokerlessThe value doubles if no jokers are anywhere in your hand, except in hands made only of singles and pairs, where jokers can’t be used anyway.

Worked examples

Using the practice hand (Consecutive Run, 25 points):

  • Win on a discard, with a joker in your hand: the discarder pays 50, the other two pay 25 each. You collect 100.
  • Self-drawn, with a joker: all three pay 50. You collect 150.
  • Jokerless, on a discard: the value doubles to 50, so the discarder pays 100 and the others 50 each. You collect 200.

Using (Singles & Pairs, 50 C): jokerless never doubles here, since jokers couldn’t be used. Won on a discard, the discarder pays 100 and the others 50: you collect 200.

Tables differ on some scoring details. You can change them in Settings.

9. Beginner strategy

  • Keep several hands open early. After the deal, find two or three hands your tiles fit, and keep tiles that serve more than one.
  • Commit around the end of the Charleston. By the courtesy pass you should know your main target, and a backup.
  • Pairs are the hard part. Jokers can’t help with pairs and singles. A hand that needs a pair of a tile that’s already been thrown three times is in trouble.
  • Hold on to jokers. They’re the most flexible tiles you have. Throwing one is rarely right.
  • Watch exposures. Once a player exposes, only a few hands on the card fit what they’ve shown. Tap an opponent in the game to see which.
  • Know when to play defense. Late in the wall, if you’re still far from a hand and someone else looks close, stop feeding them: discard tiles they can’t use, even if it hurts your hand. Throwing someone’s winning tile costs you double.
